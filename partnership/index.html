04 · "How does it actually make money?"
Five streams, and deliberately not the exchange rate.

The obvious assumption is that a remittance business lives on FX spread. Ours does not, by design. Spread is the most contested, least defensible line in cross-border, and it varies by corridor and by bank. Revenue is built on the workflow instead.

PRIMARY
Platform fee
A fee per successfully completed transaction. Predictable for the bank to budget, and it scales with volume rather than with pricing aggression.
SECONDARY
Bank revenue share
A share of net FX and fee revenue above an agreed threshold, which aligns us with the bank's economics instead of competing against them.
PREMIUM
Assisted service
A fee for high-touch cases, medical and immigration in particular, where urgency and documentation complexity justify hands-on support.
RECURRING
B2B SaaS
Platform fees for bank and partner dashboards. Contracted, recurring revenue that does not depend on transaction volume at all.
ECOSYSTEM
Referral
Fees from banks, insurers, travel and education partners, where legally permitted and disclosed to the customer.

And the underlying ticket is large enough that this works without consumer-scale volume:

~$2,340
Average annual education remittance per Indian student abroad. Derived: RBI's US$2.92B studies-abroad total[2] divided by the MEA's estimate of 1.25M students[4]. A Money2Abroad calculation, not a published figure.
Recurring
Repeat is structural, not a retention campaign. Tuition arrives every term, accommodation and living costs every month, family maintenance every month, for as long as the student is enrolled or the dependant is abroad.

FX execution and pricing. No currency position, no treasury exposure, no rate risk on our books.

FEMA and LRS eligibility. The regulated decision, and the liability that attaches to it, sits with the bank.
RBI reporting and STR filing. Statutory reporting duties remain the authorised dealer's throughout.
06 · "What have you actually built?"
An engine that turns intent into a bank-ready case.

A customer arrives with a purpose and a folder of documents. The bank needs a structured, verified, auditable file. Five stages close that gap, and every one of them is machine work that is manual today.

STAGE 01
Onboard & classify purpose
Consent, PAN and passport profile, beneficiary capture. The purpose engine maps intent to the exact fields, documents and checklist that purpose requires.
STAGE 02
Extract
OCR and document intelligence across passport, visa, admission letters, university and medical invoices, relationship proof. Fields extracted, document type classified.
STAGE 03
Validate
Name mismatches, expired documents, missing pages, invoice totals reconciled against the remittance amount, inconsistent metadata flagged.
STAGE 04
Score & triage
Risk tiering on amount, corridor, recipient, purpose, document confidence and screening output. Low, medium and high routed differently.
STAGE 05
Hand off
A structured case file with completeness score, purpose code, extracted fields with confidence, exceptions and a full audit trail, into the bank's reviewer queue.

The governance is the product. A bank cannot adopt a black box, so the constraints are built in rather than bolted on:

No black-box approval. The engine prepares and flags. The AD-I bank decides, every time.
Explainable output. Every extracted field carries a confidence score and a model version.
Human review on exceptions. Anything uncertain routes to a person before it reaches the bank.
Immutable audit trail. Every action, extraction, access and status change is logged and tamper-evident.
07 · "Why won't the bank just build this?"
Because the hard part isn't the software.

Banks own the licence, the FX and the balance sheet. What they have not built, in twenty years of trying, is a paperless purpose-code and document workflow with distribution attached to it. Four things compound in our favour.

Bank integration depth

Each live AD-I integration is months of workflow, compliance and security review. Once embedded in a bank's operations, replacement cost is high and switching is disruptive.

India-specific rule engine

Purpose codes, Form A2, relationship proof, per-country document rules. This is narrow, unglamorous domain logic that global platforms have no incentive to build for one market.

Partner distribution

Counsellors, FFMCs, CAs, travel and medical facilitators already sit between the customer and the transfer. Those relationships are held one at a time and are not purchasable at scale.

Compliance data flywheel

Every processed case improves document classification, completeness scoring and rejection prediction. Accuracy compounds with volume, and the bank sees it as falling rework.

08 · "What if the rails get faster?"
The rails are already fast. That is the point.

The instinctive fear is that faster, cheaper settlement makes an intermediary redundant. The data says settlement was solved some time ago:

92%
Of cross-border payments over SWIFT gpi credited to the beneficiary within 24 hours[15]
40%
Credited within 30 minutes[15]

So when an Indian customer's outward transfer takes days, almost none of that is the rail. It is document collection, purpose classification, missing paperwork, resubmission and manual review. The friction we remove and the friction the rails removed are two different problems.

Implication 1
Faster rails increase our value
As settlement time approaches zero, paperwork becomes effectively 100% of the remaining delay. The workflow layer does not get disintermediated by speed, it gets more exposed as the binding constraint.
Implication 2
Rails commoditise, compliance does not
Settlement infrastructure competes toward zero margin. Purpose codes, Form A2, relationship evidence and per-country document rules are legal obligations that no amount of infrastructure competition removes.

Which is also the answer to "why now". Every input this business needs has only recently arrived at once: settlement is fast and traceable, document intelligence is finally accurate enough to trust with passports and invoices, the outward market has normalised at roughly US$30B after its post-COVID swing, and 1.25M Indian students are abroad generating recurring, documentation-heavy payments[4]. The workflow is the last piece still done on paper.

What could go wrong
The three risks we are underwriting.

Named plainly, because a committee will find them anyway.

Bank integration cycles are slow.
Mitigated by design: we start with a semi-integrated workflow and an operations dashboard that needs no core-banking change, and move to deep API integration only once volume justifies it.
Regulatory boundary ambiguity.
FX execution, LRS eligibility and final approval stay with the AD-I bank in every scenario. We are a preparation and workflow layer, with counsel review and explicit customer disclosure that the bank executes the remittance.
Price competition compresses yield.
We do not compete on headline FX rate, and revenue is not spread-dependent. The wedge is completion, turnaround and documentation quality in categories where a failed or delayed transfer carries real consequence, tuition deadlines, visa filings, hospital deposits.
Counterparty concentration on one bank.
The most material risk in the model, and it is real while a single AD-I partner carries every transaction. Two things limit it: the platform is bank-agnostic by construction, since purpose rules and document logic are ours rather than the bank's, and multi-bank routing is an explicit deliverable of the scale phase rather than a later idea.
